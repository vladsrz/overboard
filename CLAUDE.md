# Overboard — agent notes

Raft sumo where knocked-out players wreck the raft for whoever is left, alone against bots or with up to three others. The game is a static site at https://overboard-game.vercel.app/ (Vercel project `playoverboard`). Rooms and shared stats run on a Cloudflare Worker in `server/`; its address is for the API, not a game page. GitHub Pages is unpublished. Change the live game (push `main`, deploy the Worker) only after the owner explicitly approves deployment; give a local test link or a `dev` preview first. For now, pushing `dev` also waits for the owner to ask, although it changes nothing live. Why: a test request does not authorize changing the live game. `README.md` is for visitors; this file is for whoever changes the game.

## The brief
**Least complicated, most fun.** Check every addition against that line. Cutting is allowed.

## Design rules
These are decisions the owner has already made, mostly by trying something and rejecting it. Do not bring a rejected version back.

- **One action button.** A separate slam key was confusing, so the slam is folded into the dash.
- **A key you were already holding does not count as a new press.** A direction held while falling in used to tip the raft the instant you hit the water and start the tip's cooldown, so the first deliberate tip did nothing. You have to let go and press again (`tiltWait` in `sim.js`).
- **A dash needs a direction.** With no movement key held, Space does nothing and uses nothing up; it used to dash wherever you last moved, which at the start of a round was toward the centre. Showing the direction on the disc or auto-aiming were the other options; the owner chose this. The first time it happens a hint says to hold a direction, so the press is not swallowed silently.
- **A key press always does the thing.** Tips used to be refused silently while a bot was tipping, which read as "sometimes works and sometimes doesn't". Never add a rule that can swallow the player's input without saying so.
- **Anything on screen explains itself while you play.** Tilt and the wave were not understood until they drew their direction on the raft.
- **The start screen says how to move, plus a small portrait-phone rotation suggestion.** Three rows of game instructions were too much text. Everything else is taught by a one-off banner the first time it happens to the player (`teach` in `game.js`). The static icon shows a dim upright phone turning toward a bright horizontal phone; a single tilted handset was unclear. It and “Rotate your phone for a better experience” sit below Start only on portrait touch screens up to 600px wide, disappear in landscape and on match results, and never block portrait play.
- **Meters ride on the thing they belong to** (above the disc, under the cursor), not in a corner. They are straight bars: a dark teal track filled in the owner's colour. Rings around the discs and pie dials were tried and rejected outright.
- **Nothing drawn on the raft is black.** Outlines, hit rings and pickup tokens are the water teal, and each token shows its name. One exception: a plank the raft pulls on its own flashes black, to tell it from one a player pulled (which flashes that player's colour).
- **The countdown is the water teal.** Black and red were both tried.
- **Tilt is shown by seven small arrows** spread wide over the raft, in the tipper's colour over a dark outline, at about 35% opacity, each nudging back and forth along its direction. Rejected on the way here: many small arrows on a moving grid, one big block arrow, the same arrow with the tipper's disc on its tail, a single small arrow, and any version that fades in and out (it looks like the tip cutting out). The dark outline is what keeps a yellow player's arrows readable.
- **Tipping is a tap, not a hold.** Hold-to-tip with turn-taking was the most complicated rule in the game.
- **Everything that happens makes a sound,** and pausing is silent at once, including the automatic pause when the window loses focus. Check sound changes in Firefox as well as Chrome: a gentle fade on pause was not enough there.
- **Bots have to look like they want something.** Standing next to each other shoving read as illogical; so did a bot with Thorns not chasing anyone.
- **One banner at a time, and on a narrow window it has its own lane.** News (a fall, a round result, the raft breaking up) shows at once; a hint waits its turn and comes back if news cut it short (`banner` in `hud.js`). Whatever sends you back to the start screen (removed from a room, lost connection, a room that is closed or full, a code that is not one) is a banner too, its first sentence as the title; it used to be a small line under the buttons and was easy to miss. Below about 1080 wide the raft is drawn lower to leave room for it under the score bar; that width is written twice, in `overboard.css` and in `draw.js`, and the two must match.
- **Teaching and action readiness belong to live play.** A round result clears queued hints, and a fall finishing afterwards must not teach the water controls or mark that hint as already taught. The action button stays disabled and dim during countdown, falling and results, regardless of cooldown; ghost cooldowns continue ticking while the round drifts to its end.
- **Phones play it too, with the same rules.** A stick that appears where the left thumb lands stands in for the direction keys (so it also tips the raft in the water), and one round button stands in for Space (Dash, Slam when charged, Wave in the water). Tapping a plank pulls it; with no hovering cursor, the aim for the wave is your last tap and starts where you went in. Touch starts from `(hover: none) and (pointer: coarse)` in `draw.js`, but what decides it is what the player uses: the first finger on the screen switches the stick and button on, the first game key switches them off (`setTouch` in `game.js`). The media query alone was not enough: Firefox's phone preview with touch simulation does not report a coarse pointer. All input goes through pointer events, so a mouse and a finger share one path. On an iPhone the stick twice stayed pushed over after the thumb had left, and the cause was never pinned down from the outside. So it is built to recover whatever the phone fails to report (see the comment above `stickTo` in `game.js`): on a touchscreen it runs on touch events and follows its finger by identity, a new thumb in the zone always takes it over, and a touch that has not moved at all for 2.5 seconds is treated as gone. Keep all three. Text selection is switched off everywhere (`-webkit-user-select` as well as `user-select`): on an iPhone a thumb resting on a label started selecting its words, which showed selection handles over the game and is the likeliest thing that swallowed that touch.
- **The final score is a list, not a sentence.** One row per player under the result, most rounds first: disc, name, the same round pips as the score bar, and the number. Names and scores run together on one line (`A 0 · B 0 · C 3`) could not be read, worst of all with names that have spaces in them.
- **The start screen has one big button.** Play online is full size; Play solo (unranked) and Create room are small outlined buttons side by side under it, and the room code is a bare underlined field, not a fourth button. Leave a clear gap between that code row and the lower control hints; keep the code prompt and quiet links readable over the raft. Data-collection copy lives on `privacy.html`, reached by a quiet Privacy link on the start screen and in the pause menu — not as a footnote on the cover.
- **The room has one big button and one line of words.** Start match is the only full-size button, and only whoever starts has it; the others get a small Sit out. Beside the room code, one small icon copies the code and one the invite link (a tick shows it worked); a separate Copy invite link button stacked over Sit out made the screen look like a pile of buttons. Reactions sit behind one small square button beside the main one and open as a panel under it, without moving anything; a row of eight outlined tags always on show was the busiest thing on the screen and the least important. Leaving is plain underlined text. Your name is typed on your own seat, not in a separate field. The first version had a name row, two full-size buttons, a boxed Close room and a two-line instruction under them, and read as too many equal choices.
- **Match point is called.** When a round begins with anyone one round from winning the match, a banner says so and names them ("You" for the player), with its own sound (a short drum roll into one hit; three square notes were replaced, and the other candidates are on `dev/sounds.html`), while the round counts in.
- **Crowns mark the match threat.** The sole round leader wears gold; when two or more players tie at match point, each wears blue. Ties below match point get no crown. Crowns sit on top of discs, clear of charge bars, and never reveal hidden opponents.
- **Keep the original spawn ring.** Discs start 150 units from the center. Tile centers, two-plank seams and four-plank corners looked awkward, so do not bring those layouts back.
- **Mute lives in the pause menu** (a speaker icon) and on `M`. There is no mute button on the play screen.

## How the rules work

**Hits.** Knockback comes from the speed you carry into the hit and grows faster than the speed does (`HIT_REF`, `HIT_POW`). The dash is a burst of thrust, not an instant kick, so it only hits hard with a run-up. Leaning on someone at close range barely moves them.

**Slam.** A bar above each disc charges over about 14 seconds (`ULT_T`). When it is full, the next dash is a slam: a blast on the first player hit, or where the dash ends. A bot's bar is only drawn once it is loaded.

**Footing and gaps.** Two forgiving rules decide when you fall. `FOOT` (7 units): you are standing as long as any plank is within that distance of your centre, so you only go in once most of your disc is over a gap. `TEETER_T` (0.22s): once you are off, you have that long to get back on. Together they mean gaps can be crossed if you are quick: a run clears one missing plank, a dash clears up to three, nothing clears four, and a slow walk falls into one. The test pins those numbers. Bots know them too (`danger()` in `bots.js`). The cost: a pulled plank only drops someone standing well inside it.

**When the raft breaks up.** There is no timer shrinking the raft. If nobody goes in the water for 8 seconds (`STALL_T`), the raft pulls its own planks, one every 0.6s (`STALL_EVERY`; it was 0.45s, turned down to three quarters of that pace when Meteors arrived; half was tried and was too slow so the big plank losses come from a pickup someone chose to grab), half the time under someone and otherwise anywhere. If nobody falls for 18 seconds, the raft pulls faster for a short burst aimed near the survivors (`STALL_METEOR_*`), with the same black one-second blink as its other pulls; it stops when someone falls. That burst was first drawn as a meteor strike (a teal blink, a target ring and a falling stone) and was rejected: it looked bad and read as a third way to lose planks, when to a player it is the raft breaking up faster. Keep it looking like the raft's own pulls. The Meteors pickup is a different thing: planks come out all over the raft in the colour of whoever grabbed it, never near them. The moment somebody falls the normal no-fall clock restarts. Once every real player is in the water, a separate finish clock starts: planks are pulled near the remaining bots after 0.5 seconds and the faster aimed burst begins after 3 seconds, continuing until the round ends. Bot falls do not restart that clock. Hazards favor the leading bot 85% of the time, and bot targeting gives more weight to bots with more wins (`4^wins`); the score is never changed directly. It was 65% while Meteors pulled about 40 planks; with the smaller shower and the easier time for people below, 65% let the trailing bot win only half the finishes, and 85% puts it at about 53% (the test wants 52–70%). In 500 simulated solo rounds the median post-player-fall wait was 6.9 seconds, 90% ended within 11.1 seconds and the longest took 16.4 seconds. Being in the water is still half the game while other people remain aboard.

**In the water.** Click pulls a plank (1s warning blink). A direction tips the raft that way for 2 seconds, then recharges for 4. One tip at a time; a person's tip always starts at once, even over a bot mid-tip, and bots take turns. The arrows keep the tipper's colour while they fade out. Space sends a wave toward the cursor.

**Pickups.** Bomb (blasts everyone near you), Big (heavier and larger), Power (hits and slam land 1.7x harder), Unseen (bots lose you; a hit reveals you briefly), Thorns (anyone who touches you is thrown back), Charge (fills the slam bar), Meteors (for 1.8 seconds planks come out all over the raft, about 18 of its 177, in the colour of whoever grabbed it and never within 115 units of them; at most one a round and not in its first 8 seconds; everyone gets a banner saying who set it off). The first pass was 2.6s / ~40 planks / 95 safe / from 6s and ended fights too often, so it was turned down. The first appears 1.5s into a round, then one every 2.5s, up to five at once. The kind is random but never the same twice in a row; duplicates on the raft are allowed. Each lands at least 150 units from the others and from the last one, and planks in the outer ring are only kept 30% of the time (`PICKUP_RIM_CHANCE`), because an even pick on a round raft put 59% of them at the rim. It is about 35% now.

## The cast
Seventeen characters, three drawn at random each match. Who they are (name, score-bar label, disc colour, hue) is in `js/cast.js`; how they play is in `js/bots.js` under the same key (`BRAINS`). The test fails if the two lists differ.

Each has a style on the raft (who it targets, how much it dashes, how badly it wants pickups, how careful it is at the edge) and a favourite thing to do in the water (tilt, waves, planks, or all). The label next to its name says which and switches when it falls in. In the water the favourite is what a bot uses first and fastest, but about half the bots (`BUSY` in `bots.js`, 55%, rolled each time one goes in) also use the other tools at a middle pace, pulling whatever plank the cursor is over as it closes on its target. The rest stick to the favourite. Favourite-only looked idle next to a person; 90% near-cooldown spam (and a 75% try) made solo rounds too short once meteors were in too, so this sits in between.

Five have a habit that is theirs alone: Jackal goes for whoever the others are already on (`pile`), Crab keeps to the middle and only fights whoever steps into it (`guard`), Dodo dashes without checking there is raft where the dash ends (`rash`, and it goes off the edge on its own about twice as often as most), Eel backs away from anyone close and waits for someone at the edge (`flee`), and Nemesis prefers a person to a bot (`people`). Their colours (white, mint, magenta, pink) are hues no other character has.

To add one, add a row in each file. `pick` takes combined traits (`'grudge near'`), and the flags `shy`, `patient` and `wild` stack with it. Names must keep different first letters, because the letter is drawn on the disc, and a match never draws two of the same `hue`.

What a bot is holding changes its mood: with Thorns it drops its plan and runs straight at the nearest player; Power, Big or a loaded slam make it press the attack.

**People who are not ahead get an easier time** (`easy()` in `bots.js`). While someone else has at least as many round wins as the person, bots weigh the person as a target at 0.3 of normal (Nemesis included), aim charges at them 2.2x looser, move a water cursor toward them at 220 instead of 380, and a bot they knocked in comes back for them 15% of the time instead of 60%. Once the person leads the match, all of that switches off. The owner asked for it to be easier to win, with bots getting tougher once you start winning. Measured with a bot in the person's seat over 600 matches (first to 3, 25% is even): an average player went from about 20% of matches to 35%, a weak one from 7% to 10%. A stronger setting (0.15 / 2.6x / 180 / 10%) gave 40% and 15%. A bot in the person's seat cannot measure this well, because bots avoid holes perfectly; play it.

A player who has not steered or dashed for 3 seconds (`AFK_T`, counted by `idleT` in `sim.js`; being shoved does not reset it) is the easy target: most bots, most of the time, drop whoever they were after and go for them, the nearest if there are several, on the raft and from the water. Each bot rolls for it (`AFK_PULL`, 0.75) when it notices and again each time it rethinks; every bot, every time, was tried and was too much. The test measures about 80% of bot-time spent on the idle player. Steering again ends it at once. Bots never sit still that long themselves, so bot-only matches are unchanged.

Bots cannot see an Unseen player, but they guess (`trackOrGuess`): a bot that was after you keeps heading for where it last saw you for 1.5 to 4 seconds depending on its skill, with a guess that drifts further off the longer you stay hidden. It never reads the hidden player's real position, so moving away shakes it off.

## With friends
`js/net.js` on the player's side, `server/` for the game server. Whoever runs the match runs `Sim.step` exactly as in a solo game and is the only authority; everyone else sends input every time it changes and gets the whole match back every other step (`Net.pack` / `Net.unpack`). An invite link is the page address plus `#` and a five-character room code.

There are two ways a room can run, and a player's side is the same code for both (`tickGuest` in `game.js`):

- **On the game server**, when `SERVER` in `net.js` is set. `server/src/worker.js` is a Cloudflare Worker; each room is one Durable Object that holds the players' WebSockets and runs the match, bots included, importing `sim.js`, `bots.js`, `cast.js` and `net.js` straight from `js/`. Nobody is the host: whoever has been in the room longest starts each match (`Net.lead`), and if they leave the next one can. A private room works like a party in a game with matchmaking (`Net.party`): everyone is in the next match by default, anyone else may press **Sit out** (and **I’m in** to undo it), and everyone is in again after each match; whoever starts never waits on anyone and may **remove** someone from the room. A first version made everyone press Ready before every match, with a way for anyone to start without whoever was not; the owner rejected it outright, including that anyone but the host could start. After a match everyone is taken back to the room after 5 seconds (not while typing a leaderboard name), where the seats say who is still there and how many matches each person has won since coming in (the room's score, gone when they leave). Every person's seat is labelled **host**, **ready** or **sitting out**: with only "sitting out" shown, a guest who was in looked unready on the host's screen. When someone leaves a room (not mid-match, where the match has its own banner), a banner says who, and who starts the matches now if it was the host. Whoever starts may also press Play again on the result. A public room still starts on its own.

**Reactions.** In any room (not solo), keys 1 to 8, the small button beside the main one on the room and result screens, or one under pause on a phone, send one of `Net.EMOTES`: four faces and four short phrases. The faces are the game's own SVGs (`FACES` in `hud.js`), drawn like the players' discs (plank disc, ink outline and features, one line weight); emoji were rejected as boring and look different on every phone. The bubble is dark with a plank outline: a plank-coloured one was hard to read. It shows for 2.6 seconds over the sender's seat in the room, and under their name on the score bar during a match and on its result. Only that list exists, so nothing typed reaches strangers; the server drops anything else and more than one per 0.8 seconds per player. A room with nobody in it shuts itself down, and one that sits without a match for 15 minutes closes, because a room costs while it is awake.
- **In the host's browser**, when `SERVER` is empty: the host's tab runs the match and the others reach it directly over WebRTC, found through PeerJS's public broker (`vendor/peerjs.min.js`, v1.5.4). The match ends for everyone if the host leaves. This was the first version and is kept as the fallback.

There are three ways into a room, all on the start screen. **Create room** opens a private room and gives an invite link; the same five-character code can be typed into **Have a room code?**, which joins on the fifth character. The big **Play online** button connects to `/quick` and puts you in a public room that is still gathering players; the `Desk` opens another if its current room is mid-match. A room also refuses a late `/quick` arrival if it started after the desk's pick, and the Worker retries with a new room. A public room has no leader and no invite link. It starts by itself 8 seconds after the first player arrives (at once when full), and again 8 seconds after each match. Someone alone gets a **Start now** button, before the first match and between matches; it disappears the moment a second person arrives, and the room refuses an early start from anyone who is not alone (`hear` in `worker.js`, `Net.lead` in `net.js`). **Play solo (unranked)** starts the old local solo match. If the big button cannot connect to the server, it starts that local match with an unranked notice. Someone who touches nothing for two minutes in a public room is let go, or a forgotten tab would keep a room running for ever.

A page opened on this machine or the home network always uses a server on the same machine at port 8787, so server changes can be tried before deploying: `npm run dev` in `server/`, serve the game folder, open it by the machine's network address.

The server is live at `overboard.overboard-server.workers.dev` on Cloudflare's free plan. It handles rooms and stats; it does not serve the game page. Deploy server changes with `npm run deploy` in `server/` (needs `npx wrangler login` once per machine). The Vercel project is connected to the GitHub repository and builds from it (`vercel.json`: it runs `node server/build-site.mjs` and publishes `server/site/`, which contains no notes, tests or server code). **`main` is exactly what is live: pushing it publishes the game page** at `overboard-game.vercel.app`. **Work happens on `dev`**; pushing `dev` changes nothing live and builds a preview at `playoverboard-git-dev-vlads-projects-674ba138.vercel.app`, which asks for the owner's Vercel sign-in. A preview is HTTPS, so it can test the Home Screen install; solo play works there but rooms do not, because the live Worker only accepts the real address (`ALLOWED_ORIGINS`). To deploy: merge `dev` into `main`, push `main`, and deploy the Worker in the same go (it is not connected to Git and shares `js/` with the page, so the two must not drift), then check the live page itself, not the build result. A Vercel rollback switches off the project's automatic domain assignment (`autoAssignCustomDomains`): after one, a production build succeeds but `overboard-game.vercel.app` keeps serving the old one. That happened on the first Git deploy; the fix is to point the alias at the new build and set the flag back to true (`vercel api /v9/projects/playoverboard -X PATCH -F autoAssignCustomDomains=true`). The manual `vercel deploy server/site --project playoverboard --prod --yes` still works but leaves `main` behind the live game; do not use it. GitHub Pages is disabled. To try the live server from a local page, add `?server=live` to the address. Pages allowed to connect are listed in `ALLOWED_ORIGINS` in `server/wrangler.toml`. The server drops a player who sends a message over 600 characters or anything that is not text, who sends more than 150 a second, or who goes quiet for 8 seconds, and seats at most four.

**What keeps strangers from running up the bill.** The `Origin` check only stops browsers on other sites: any script can claim to be a local page, so every route is treated as open to scripts. One source (the caller's address, hashed and never stored; an IPv6 address is cut to its /64) may connect 30 times a minute across `/new`, `/join` and `/quick` and report 20 times a minute to `/stat`, counted by Cloudflare's rate limiting (`CONNECTS` and `REPORTS` in `server/wrangler.toml`) before any room wakes. A whole school or phone network can share one address, so raise those numbers if honest players start getting "Could not open a room". If the limiter is missing the server lets everyone in rather than nobody. A report over 16 KB is refused unread, a round may hold at most 80 pickups, and the statistics keep the newest 20,000 matches and 400 days of daily rows. One source may also hold at most 8 connections open at once (`HOLD` in `worker.js`), counted by the `Desk` object, because an open connection keeps its room awake. A turned-away player is told why: "too many tries" or "too many games open from your network". The count lives only in the Desk's memory and rebuilds itself: rooms report who they hold on every arrival and departure and once a minute.

What the free plan really runs out of is awake rooms, not requests. Cloudflare bills a room for every second it has a connection open, at 128 MB, and the free plan gives 13,000 GB-s a day: about 29 room-hours a day across everyone, reset at 00:00 UTC. Past that, rooms fail until the reset; solo play is unaffected and nothing is charged. Inactivity timers do not help against a script, which can fake activity; only the per-source caps do. A determined person with many addresses can still use up a day's allowance. Moving to the paid plan removes that ceiling but turns the same attack into a bill, so stay on the free plan until real play gets near the limit. A refused request still counts toward the 100,000 a day.

**The site's headers** are in `vercel.json` and only exist on Vercel; a local static server sends none. The policy allows scripts from the site itself and nothing inline, so a page added to `build-site.mjs` must load its script from a file (`dev/cast.html`, `dev/sounds.html` and `dev/og.html` still have inline ones and are not published). It also forbids showing the game inside a frame on another site, and allows fonts only from the site itself: the two typefaces are files in `fonts/`, declared in `css/fonts.css`, not fetched from Google, so a visitor's browser contacts nobody else. The server's address is now written in three places that must change together: `SERVER` in `js/net.js`, `ALLOWED_ORIGINS` in `server/wrangler.toml` and `connect-src` in `vercel.json`. If rooms ever fall back to the host's browser, add `https://0.peerjs.com wss://0.peerjs.com` to `connect-src`. A preview build logs one blocked script, Vercel's own toolbar; leave it blocked.

- **Seats.** People sit in the order they joined, host first, in the colours of `Cast.PEOPLE`; bots fill the rest up to four, never in a person's hue. `G.me` is this machine's seat. `p.human` means "a person", not "you": anything about the local player compares against `G.me`.
- **Guest input prediction is off by default.** `tickGuest` in `game.js` keeps the acknowledged input replay for comparison; `?predict=1` enables it. By default, guests show the latest server snapshot without replaying local or stale inputs. Other players still use their last reported steering; sounds and results come only from the server.
- **What happened** (sounds, rings, banners) comes from the host's event list, sent with the snapshot. A guest throws away the events of its own in-between steps, or everything would sound twice.
- **Nothing from the wire is trusted.** The host reads a name and an input from a guest and clamps both; a guest forces every field of a snapshot into the type the match already holds and refuses seat numbers that do not exist. Names go to the page as text, never as HTML. Messages can arrive out of order, so each kind carries a counter.
- **Nobody can pause a shared match.** Esc opens the menu over a match that carries on. A hidden tab gets no animation frames, so a worker's timer carries the match while the tab is hidden (`pump` in `game.js`); without it a host who switched tabs would freeze everyone.
- **Leaving.** A player who leaves or goes quiet becomes a bot in the same seat. In a browser-hosted room, the host leaving ends it and guests go back to the start screen with a line saying so. Someone who joins while a match is on waits for the next one.
- **Limits to know about.** The room code is the only lock. On the server, Cloudflare's free plan allows 100,000 requests a day (an incoming message counts as a twentieth of one) and fails closed past that, which is why input is only sent when it changes. In a browser-hosted room players connect directly, so they can see each other's IP addresses, and rooms cannot be opened while the public broker is down.

To check it: run the local server, serve the folder, open the page in two separate browser profiles, open a room in one and the invite link in the other. The node test covers the snapshot round trip only. Python's `http.server` drops requests when two pages load at once, which looks like a broken game ("Sim is not defined"); use `npx http-server . -c-1`.

## Play stats
`js/stats.js` keeps a record of every match played on a device, in that browser's localStorage (`overboard-stats`, the last 400 matches). No names are kept: a seat is `person` or a bot's key. Per round it holds the length, the winner, every fall (who, by whom, how, when), every pickup taken and whether the raft pulled its own planks. A local match is written after each round, so an abandoned one still counts as started.

`dev/stats.html` reads that local record and shows it as plain tables (win rates by mode, round lengths, falls by cause, each bot's record against the player, pickups), with an export to JSON. It only sees matches played on the same device at the same address, because a browser keeps a separate store per address.

The server now gathers shared play stats in a `Stats` Durable Object (`server/src/stats.js`). A browser reports visits and finished solo matches; the room server reports private and public matches, including ones abandoned after a round. The shared record has match events, a random per-browser ID, country and device type; it has no names or IP addresses. `privacy.html` discloses this; the start screen and pause menu only link to that page. `dev/players.html` shows counts and play patterns across devices after a statistics key is entered. The key is a Wrangler `STATS_KEY` secret, never a client-side constant; the page keeps it only in sessionStorage. The Cloudflare site build includes that page. The report endpoint validates and limits input, and browser-reported solo results can be falsified, so these counts are for tuning, not prizes or rankings.

For numbers that do not depend on anyone playing, `node test/sim.test.js` already prints bot-against-bot statistics.

## Leaderboard
`leaderboard.html` (with `js/leaderboard.js`) lists this week's match wins; a week starts Monday 00:00 UTC and the page gives that in the reader's own time. The owner chose three things: **only matches the game server ran count** (a private room or Play online, against people or bots), because Start match runs in the browser and its result could be made up; **ranked by match wins this week**, a tie going to whoever got there first; and **nobody is listed until they add a name**, which they can only do once they have a win.

- **Where a win comes from.** When a room's match ends, `Room.credit` posts the winner's browser id (the made-up one from `Stats.device()`, sent with the player's name card) to the `Stats` object's internal `/win`. The Worker never routes `/win`, so no request from outside can add one. A seat whose player left is a bot by the end and credits nobody.
- **What a browser can do.** `GET /board` (top 20 names), `POST /board/me` (its own wins, name and place) and `POST /board/name` (add or change its name, or `remove: true`), all by its id and all under the `REPORTS` limit. The id is the only key: whoever has it can rename that entry, which is why it is never shown anywhere.
- **Bot-only wins.** A room with only one person may credit at most ten match wins per browser id per UTC day (`bot_wins` in `Stats`). Matches with another person still count. The cap is tied to the made-up browser id, so clearing site data or using a new id can get around it; there is no account identity to enforce a per-human limit.
- **Names** get the room's clean-up (`Net.cleanName`) in the Worker and are checked again in `Stats` (`boardName`): at most 12 letters, digits and a little punctuation, and nothing `rude()` lets through, private room or not, because strangers read the board.
- **Moderation.** The owner-only `dev/players.html` lists names and can remove one by its browser id through `POST /admin/remove-name`, protected by `STATS_KEY`. That id goes on `blocked_names` and cannot add another name. `GET /admin/names` and the removal response are marked `no-store`; neither route reveals ids publicly. A player may still remove their own name on `leaderboard.html` without being blocked.
- **Kept** eight weeks of wins (`BOARD_WEEKS`); a name is deleted with its last win. `privacy.html` says this.
- **In the game.** After a match won in a room, the end screen asks "Put your name on this week's leaderboard?" with the room name filled in (`offerBoard` in `hud.js`), about a second later so the server has counted the win. "No thanks" is remembered in this browser and it does not ask again; the page still offers it. A player whose name is up gets one line with their place instead. The start screen has a quiet Leaderboard link next to Privacy, and the week's top five as small planks (`miniBoard` in `hud.js`): in the top-right corner where it clears the big title (`placeMini` measures; a 1280x800 window puts the title too high), so the middle keeps its one big button; otherwise under the links; left out where neither fits; and not drawn at all while nobody is on the board. `GET /board` may be kept by a browser for 30 seconds, so every start screen does not wake the server; the leaderboard page asks fresh.
- `node server/test.mjs` covers the week boundary, names, ranking and ties, taking a name off, the weekly reset, pruning, the bot-only cap, moderation access, and which seat a room credits.

## Phone layout and Home Screen app

Portrait and landscape are supported. CSS uses safe-area insets for the score, banners, controls and covers; `draw.js` reads the body's resolved safe-area padding to fit and centre the raft in the same area. Landscape reserves thumb lanes on each side. Keep those lane widths in sync with `.stick-zone`. Short screens use a compact, scrollable pause menu. Resizing clears held input before redrawing, so rotation cannot carry a stale thumb hold into the new orientation; retain the three existing lost-touch recovery paths.

`manifest.webmanifest` uses `display: standalone` and `orientation: any`, with relative start URL and scope for Vercel. The Apple web-app metadata in `index.html` supports Home Screen launch. This hides Safari's toolbar when opened from the Home Screen icon; system status and home indicators are controlled by iOS. The pause menu gives installation instructions only on touch devices outside standalone mode. No service worker is used, so this is not offline support.

On the first Start press per page load (not Start now in a public room, which would start under it), touch players in the browser get a short Home Screen guide with Continue in browser and Back. iPhone/iPad get Safari's Share instructions; other touch devices get browser-menu instructions. Home Screen and desktop launches start immediately. The guide is a native modal dialog, and Enter, Escape, Back, replay and restart must keep their normal behavior. Detect the launch mode with `display-mode: standalone` or Apple's `navigator.standalone`, not screen-height guesses about Safari's collapsed toolbar.

Test Home Screen launch over HTTPS. A LAN HTTP preview played in Safari but its Home Screen launch failed with “HTTP URL with HTTPS-Only enabled”; browser play alone does not verify installation. For an HTTPS tunnel, serve a temporary copy of only the game assets, never the repository root, so `.git` and agent notes are not exposed. A plain LAN preview is still useful for layout and controls.

`apple-touch-icon.png` (180), `icon-192.png` and `icon-512.png` are browser renders of `favicon.svg` at their named sizes, with the water colour as the full square background. Re-render all three after an icon change.

## Before calling a change done
1. `node test/sim.test.js`. Read the "by character" line after touching a brain: each character plays 32 matches, so about 8 wins is even. The numbers are noisy at that size (the same brains have ranged from 3 to 13 between runs), so only react to one that is far out.
2. Open `index.html` and play the part you changed. A browser caches the scripts hard when served from a local server, so reload without cache.
3. `js/sim.js` stays pure: no DOM, no clock, no `Math.random`. A new rule gets a test.
